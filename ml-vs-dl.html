Title

Machine Learning or Deep Learning? A Decision Guide for AI Projects

A comparison of traditional machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) through two real-world case studies: predicting house prices with linear regression, and recognizing faces with a convolutional neural network. I adapted my course report into a short guide and an interactive checklist that decision makers can apply to their own projects.

Objective

Many organizations assume the newest, most complex AI is always the best choice. In my experience, that leads to expensive models that are hard to explain and no more accurate than simpler ones. My goal was to show, with concrete examples, how the type of data, the amount of data, and the need for explanation should drive the choice.

Case studies
Machine learning

House price prediction

Approach: linear regression on features such as bedrooms, square footage, and neighborhood.

Why it fits: the data is a structured table, experts already know which features matter, a few thousand sales are enough, and each coefficient can be explained to a homeowner, lender, or tax assessor.

Why not deep learning: too little data, no hidden patterns in raw signals to discover, and a black-box answer is hard to defend. Tree-based models still often beat deep learning on typical tabular data (Grinsztajn et al., 2022).

Deep learning

Facial recognition

Approach: a convolutional neural network learning directly from pixels.

Why it fits: one 224 × 224 color image holds 150,528 numbers with no meaning on their own. The network learns edges, then shapes, then identities (LeCun et al., 2015), and large labeled image sets exist.

Why not traditional ML: hand-measured features like eye distance break when lighting, angle, or expression changes.

Process
  1. Analyze: studied each example from the course lesson and identified the deciding factors: data type, data volume, computing cost, and explainability.
  2. Argue both sides: for each case, explained why the chosen approach fits and why the other one would fail, which is a stronger test than listing advantages.
  3. Verify with research: supported the claims with peer-reviewed sources, including a NIST evaluation of demographic effects in face recognition.
  4. Adapt for the audience: turned a 6-page academic report into a scannable guide and an interactive three-question checklist for managers and project leads.

Value
For managers and teams planning an AI investment, this guide helps avoid a costly mistake: choosing deep learning when a simpler, cheaper, and more explainable model would do the job. It shows that I can match an AI technique to a business problem and explain the trade-offs in plain language.
Key insights
  • Start with the simplest model that fits the data and the need for explanation.
  • Move to deep learning only when the data is unstructured, plentiful, and too complex for human-designed features.
  • Powerful models bring responsibilities: a U.S. government evaluation found that many face recognition algorithms had higher error rates for some demographic groups (Grother et al., 2019), so testing for bias and keeping humans in high-stakes decisions is essential.
  • My own research followed the same principle: with limited, expensive data, I built known physics into smaller machine learning models instead of reaching for the largest network.
